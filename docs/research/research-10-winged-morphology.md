# Winged animals at rest: bird, bat and gliding mammal

*A point-in-time research dossier gathered for the proposed third (winged) family (Overlands #1859, 2026-11-05; its companion on locomotion and flight is research-11). It records what was found and why the recommendation in section 7 was made. Every figure carries its own confidence. A value read only in a search snippet, recalled from a paywalled paper, or derived here is marked so and needs checking before anything hard-codes it.*

Research for a procedural rig of winged animals: bone ratios, fold angles, surfaces, faces and rest postures. Gaits and flight kinematics are out of scope.

Conventions used throughout:
- **HUM** means humerus greatest length. Wing bones are given against HUM, because the humerus is the one segment every flyer has in a comparable form.
- **FA** means forearm length (bats and gliders, measured on the skin over the radius, as in field guides).
- **HB** means head-and-body length, nose to the base of the tail, as museum and field accounts give it.
- **Interior angle** is the angle between two bone axes at the joint: 180 deg is straight, smaller is more folded. Where a source uses another convention, the row says so.
- **Confidence**: H means a measured value from a peer-reviewed study or a measured specimen read in this session. M means measured but with a caveat about method or sample (a search snippet of a measured source, a small sample, a fact sheet). L means a rule of thumb, a figure derived here from other rows (marked "derived"), or background textbook knowledge not verified in this session (marked "bg").
- **Source keys** (S1, S2, ...) point to section 9.

## 1. Summary: ten facts that most change how convincing a winged body looks

1. **A bird's wing is mostly feather, not bone.** A carrion crow's wing bones total about 23 cm per side (humerus 68, ulna 83, carpometacarpus 50 mm, digits about 30 mm; S1), yet its span is about 98 cm (S4). With about 10 cm of body between the shoulders, the primaries carry the tip about 20 cm past the last bone: roughly 40% of the span (derived, L). A rig that sizes the wing from bones alone looks like a plucked chicken.
2. **In a bat, the reverse is true: the bones are the wing's frame and its outline.** In the large flying fox, FA is 179 to 220 mm, the third metacarpal 123 to 145 mm and that digit's first phalanx 88 to 110 mm (S20). The membrane's edge runs from fingertip to fingertip and back to the ankle, so the skeleton fixes the outline at every frame.
3. **The bird's elbow and wrist move together.** In all 61 species measured, flexing the elbow flexes the wrist through a linkage (S6). A folded bird wing is a Z: the humerus points back along the flank, the forearm points forward, and the hand points back again. Crown-bird fossils, which preserve the fold, show a median elbow interior angle of 31 deg and a wrist of 52 deg (S5). One "fold" parameter should drive both joints.
4. **A bird's trunk is rigid, and its neck does all the moving.** The synsacrum fuses the last thoracic, lumbar, sacral and first caudal vertebrae with the pelvis (S41). Many birds also fuse 2 to 5 thoracic vertebrae into a notarium (S41). Neck counts run from about 10 (parrots) to 25 or 26 (swans) (S11), with 14 in owls (S10). Bend the bird at the neck and at the hip joint, never along the back.
5. **Bird eyes barely move, so the head does the looking.** A barn owl's eyes move only 1 to 4 deg (S12). A starling's move up to 32 deg and a peafowl's up to 55 deg, yet head turns still dominate gaze in birds; in pigeons the eyes supply only 10 to 20% of gaze changes (S12). Owls turn their heads "up to 270 degrees in either direction" in press reports of the Johns Hopkins study (S10). An idle bird animates as a series of quick head saccades, each one a held pose.
6. **The bat's leg is turned round.** The hindlimb is rotated 90 deg or more from the ancestral mammal position. The knee faces dorsolaterally, and flexing it moves the foot ventrally (S22). A bat crawling or hanging uses a leg that bends "the wrong way" compared with the glider's.
7. **The bat and the sugar glider share one membrane line.** A bat's plagiopatagium runs from the fifth digit to the hindlimb (S25), anchored at the ankle in fruit bats (S22). The sugar glider's patagium runs from the outer side of the forefoot (the fifth finger) to the ankle (S30, S31). A flying squirrel's patagium starts at the wrist instead, held out by an extra styliform cartilage (S35, S37). The digit V to ankle edge is a parameter the bat and glider corners can share; the flying squirrel would need an extra bone.
8. **Membranes are thin, taut and elastic.** Bat wing skin is about an order of magnitude thinner than body skin (S23), around 130 to 300 micrometres (S24). Its elastin bundles run spanwise (proximodistally) and take up the slack when the wing folds, and the plagiopatagium holds five arrays of muscle (S23). When the wing folds, the membrane bunches between the digits and does not sag.
9. **At rest, a sugar glider reads as a quadruped.** HB is 115 to 210 mm, the tail is 150 to 210 mm and the mass is 60 to 160 g (S30). The patagium folds against the flank, so at rest the visible signals are the big eyes, the dorsal stripe and a tail as long as the body. In flight it glides at a ratio of 1.8 to 2.2 (26 to 30 deg below horizontal) (S32).
10. **The young are the cute end of each axis.** Newborn bats weigh 17.5% of the mother's mass in fruit bats and 21 to 29% in other families (S26), so a juvenile bat is big-headed and short-winged. Owlets and fledglings show the same baby schema (large head, large eyes, short face) that research-07 measured for puppies and kittens (S38). A "cute" default should push head and eye size and shorten the face and bill, not shrink the wings.

How the remiges overlap: seen from above, each flight feather overlies the next one toward the wingtip, and the inner vane of each lies under its neighbour (bg, consistent with S45's tertial-on-top rule). When the wing spreads, the linkage of fact 3 moves the feathers' bases apart and the fan opens evenly, the primaries most. When it folds, the feathers slide over one another into a stack of about one primary's width (L).

## 7. Recommendation

### 7.1 Do the three corners blend?

### 7.2 Choices

1. **Keep three corners, with these references:**
   - **Bird: a small owl** (little owl, *Athene noctua*, or a scops owl) for the cute default. Keep the corvid as the skeleton reference for wing ratios, and treat crow, parrot and songbird as presets inside the bird corner. An owl's round head, forward eyes and facial disc read at 10 m better than any other bird feature.
   - **Bat: a fruit bat.** Keep *Pteropus* for its ratios, but use a smaller fox-faced fruit bat (for example *Rousettus* or *Cynopterus*) as the cute default. Its large eyes and simple ears look friendlier than a microbat's noseleaf.
   - **Glider: the sugar glider, not the flying squirrel.** Its patagium starts at digit V, the bat's own trailing-edge digit, so the membrane edge blends between them with no extra bone. The flying squirrel's styliform cartilage would add a bone to the rig. The sugar glider is also the cuter, more famous animal.
2. **Do not add a pterosaur.** Its membrane hangs from a single elongated digit IV, so it would duplicate the bat's role. It is extinct, so it has no rest-posture data, and it is hard to make cute.
3. **Watch the glider's overlap with the quadrupeds.** If the owner wants every corner to read as "winged" at rest, the glider is the weak one. One way out is to put the membrane at the glider corner so it is always visible: a slight fold that stays out at rest, a convention of stylisation rather than anatomy.
4. **Family name: "Winged" in the UI, `Alate` in code.** "Avian" excludes bats and gliders. "Volant" means capable of true flight in most zoological use, so it excludes gliders. "Alate" means "having wings or wing-like expansions" and is used for membranous wings in botany and entomology, so it covers the patagium. "Winged" is the plain product word; it matches the repo's rule that UI strings use everyday words.

## 8. Notes: what the numbers do not capture

- **Feathers change the outline as much as the bones.** Fluffing, sleeking and the fold's position decide whether a bird looks round or slim. A procedural bird needs a "plumage volume" axis separate from body size.
- **The bird's visible leg starts at the knee's level.** Thigh and knee are under the flank feathers, so the joint an observer reads as the "knee" is the ankle (the joint between tibiotarsus and tarsometatarsus), bending backwards.
- **Bat fingers need a "slack" pass.** Membrane between folded digits should bunch along spanwise lines (the elastin direction), not sag.
- **Scale.** All three reference animals are small: a crow weighs 0.5 kg, a flying fox 1 kg and a sugar glider 0.1 kg. A game avatar the size of a person will need stylised mass, and its wing loading will be unrealistic. That is a design choice, not a data gap.
- **Not verified this session:** the corvid femur and tibiotarsus lengths, *Pteropus* humerus and digit V lengths, bat fold angles, and every "bg" row. A measured skeleton (the Weeks et al. skeletal dataset for passerines, S47; the Grossnickle glider table, S36) would settle the first two before anything hard-codes them.
